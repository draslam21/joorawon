Pain Shooting Down Your Leg? Sciatica Explained: Symptoms, Causes & Treatment

Sciatica is pain that travels from the lower back or buttock down one leg. It is common, it is usually not dangerous, and most people recover within a few weeks without surgery.

What Is Sciatica?

The sciatic nerve is the longest nerve in the body. It starts in the lower back, passes through the buttock and runs down the back of each leg to the foot. Sciatica is not a disease in itself. It is the name for pain caused by irritation or pressure on this nerve or on its roots in the spine.

Typical features:

  • Pain that starts in the lower back or buttock and shoots down the back or side of one leg, sometimes as far as the foot.
  • A burning, electric or stabbing quality, often different from ordinary muscle ache.
  • Tingling (“pins and needles”), numbness or a feeling of weakness in the leg or foot.
  • Pain that gets worse with coughing, sneezing, bending forward or sitting for long periods.
  • Usually one side only.

The leg pain is often worse than the back pain, and in some people there is no back pain at all.

What Causes It?

The most common cause is a slipped (herniated) disc. The discs between the bones of the spine act as cushions. When part of a disc bulges out, it can press on a nearby nerve root and trigger pain down the leg.

Other causes include:

  • Narrowing of the spinal canal (spinal stenosis), more common after age 50, where the space around the nerves becomes tight because of age-related wear.
  • Wear and tear of the spine (degenerative changes), including bony spurs that press on a nerve. See also osteoarthritis: a guide for everyone.
  • A vertebra slipping forward over the one below it (spondylolisthesis).
  • Tight buttock muscles (piriformis syndrome), a less common cause.
  • Pregnancy, because of weight gain, posture changes and pressure in the pelvis.
  • Injury, or very rarely, infection or a tumour pressing on the nerve.

Risk factors include age (typically 30 to 60 years), a physically demanding job or heavy lifting, long hours of sitting, smoking, excess weight and diabetes. Many people never find out exactly which cause applies to them, and that is not a problem: treatment is mostly the same.

Warning Signs: When to Seek Urgent Care

Most sciatica is not an emergency. A few symptoms, however, suggest serious pressure on the nerves and need same-day medical attention (go to the emergency department):

  • 🔴 Numbness around the genitals, anus or inner thighs (“saddle” numbness).
  • 🔴 Difficulty passing urine, loss of bladder or bowel control, or new constipation with back and leg pain.
  • 🔴 Weakness or numbness in both legs, or a leg that is rapidly becoming weaker.
  • 🔴 A foot that drags or “slaps” the floor (foot drop).
  • 🔴 Severe back pain after a fall or accident.
  • 🔴 Back pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, a past history of cancer, or a weakened immune system.
  • 🔴 Pain so severe that it is not eased at all by rest or by simple painkillers.

These signs can point to a rare but serious condition called cauda equina syndrome, where delay in treatment can lead to permanent damage.

When to See a Doctor, and What to Expect

Book an appointment if the pain has not clearly improved after 1 to 2 weeks, if it is stopping you from sleeping or working, or if you notice any numbness or weakness in the leg.

In most cases the diagnosis is made from your story and an examination. The doctor will ask where the pain travels, what makes it better or worse, and will check your reflexes, muscle strength, sensation and how you walk. A simple test of raising the straightened leg while you lie down can reproduce the pain.

Scans are not needed for most people in the first weeks, because the findings often do not change the treatment and many people without symptoms also have disc changes on MRI. An MRI is considered when there are warning signs, when weakness is getting worse, or when pain persists beyond about 6 weeks and an injection or surgery is being considered.

Treatment: What Helps and What Does Not

Most sciatica improves on its own. About 7 in 10 people feel much better within 6 to 12 weeks. The aim of treatment is to keep you comfortable and moving while the nerve settles.

What helps

  • Stay active. Gentle walking and normal daily activities speed recovery. Long bed rest makes the pain last longer.
  • Heat or cold. A warm pack or a hot shower can ease muscle spasm. Use whichever feels better.
  • Change position often. Avoid sitting for more than 30 to 45 minutes at a time, and find positions that ease the leg pain.
  • Physiotherapy. A guided programme of stretching and strengthening exercises helps many people, especially when pain lingers.
  • Anti-inflammatory tablets (such as ibuprofen), for a short period, can help some people. They are not suitable for everyone, particularly those with kidney disease, stomach ulcers, heart disease or on blood thinners, so check with a doctor or pharmacist first.
  • Nerve pain medicines may be prescribed by a doctor when pain is severe. They help some people, but not all, and can cause drowsiness or dizziness. See also what is nerve pain.

Further options when pain persists

  • Epidural steroid injection. A steroid injection near the irritated nerve can bring short-term relief, giving time for the nerve to recover.
  • Surgery. Removing the part of the disc pressing on the nerve is considered when leg pain remains severe after 6 to 12 weeks of treatment, or earlier if there is significant weakness or the warning signs above.

What to avoid

  • Prolonged bed rest.
  • Strong opioid painkillers, which add risks and do not help nerve pain well.
  • Heavy lifting and twisting movements while the pain is severe.
  • Unproven expensive treatments or aggressive spinal manipulation without a proper assessment.

Preventing Sciatica and Recurrences

Sciatica can come back, but a few habits lower the risk:

  • Stay physically active every week. Walking, swimming and cycling are gentle on the back.
  • Strengthen the muscles of your abdomen, back and hips. Core exercises support the spine.
  • Lift with your knees bent, keep the load close to your body and avoid twisting while carrying.
  • Break up long periods of sitting, and set up your chair, desk and screen so your back is supported.
  • Keep a healthy weight, since extra weight increases the load on the lower spine.
  • Do not smoke. Smoking reduces blood flow to the discs and slows healing.
  • Sleep on a mattress that is comfortable for you. Lying on your side with a pillow between the knees can ease the pressure.

Key Points

  • Sciatica is leg pain, often with back pain, caused by an irritated nerve in the lower spine.
  • Most people recover with time, activity and simple measures, without surgery.
  • Do not rest in bed for days. Keep moving as comfort allows.
  • Seek urgent care for numbness around the genitals, loss of bladder or bowel control, or rapidly worsening leg weakness.
  • See a doctor if the pain has not improved after 1 to 2 weeks or if it is severe.
