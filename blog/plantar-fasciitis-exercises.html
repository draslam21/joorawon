Exercises for Plantar Fasciitis: Stretches, Heel Raises and Foot Strengthening

If your first steps out of bed bring a sharp pain in the heel, you are in good company. Plantar fasciitis is one of the most common causes of heel pain, and it affects runners, people who stand all day, and many people who are neither. The good news is that most cases improve with consistent, targeted exercise and sensible load management, usually over several months rather than days. Here is a practical routine you can follow at home.

What Is Going On in Your Foot?

The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue running from the heel bone to the base of the toes, supporting the arch of the foot. Repeated overload, from a sudden increase in activity, long hours of standing, unsupportive footwear, tight calves or extra weight, can irritate the place where it attaches to the heel. The classic sign is pain with the first steps in the morning, or after sitting for a while, which often eases as you warm up and then returns after long periods on your feet.

What to Expect From These Exercises

Be realistic. Studies of exercise for plantar fasciitis are small, and a recent review rated the certainty of the evidence for stretching alone as low. Exercises work best as one part of treatment, together with supportive footwear, a temporary reduction in the activities that aggravate the pain, and sometimes insoles or a night splint. One randomised trial found that slow, heavy heel raises gave better results at 3 months than plantar fascia stretching, although the two groups were similar by 6 and 12 months. In practice, a mix of both is sensible. Give the routine at least 6 to 12 weeks before judging it, and keep going even once the pain eases.

1. Plantar Fascia Stretch

This stretch targets the fascia itself. Do it before your very first steps in the morning and before standing up after a long period of sitting.

  1. Sit and cross the sore foot over the opposite knee.
  2. Hold your toes and pull them back toward your shin until you feel a stretch along the arch. You can feel the fascia tighten with your other thumb.
  3. Hold for 10 seconds, then relax.
  • How often: repeat 10 times, up to 3 times a day.

2. Calf Stretches

Tight calves increase the pull through the fascia with every step, so this stretch matters even though the pain is under the heel.

  1. Stand facing a wall with the sore leg behind you, heel flat on the floor and toes pointing forward.
  2. Keep the back knee straight and lean forward until you feel a stretch in the upper calf. Hold for 30 seconds.
  3. Now bend the back knee slightly, keeping the heel down, to reach the deeper calf muscle just above the heel. Hold for another 30 seconds.
  • How often: 2 to 3 repetitions on each leg, once or twice a day.

3. Towel or Band Stretch

A towel, a strap or an elastic band lets you hold a steady stretch of the sole and the calf together.

  1. Sit with your leg straight in front of you.
  2. Loop the towel or band around the ball of the foot and hold both ends.
  3. Gently pull so that your toes and foot come toward you, keeping the knee straight, until you feel a stretch along the sole and the back of the leg.
  4. Hold for 30 seconds.
  • How often: 3 repetitions on each foot, once or twice a day.

4. Slow Heel Raises With a Rolled Towel

This is the main strengthening exercise. It loads the plantar fascia and the calf progressively, which helps the tissue become tougher and more tolerant of daily load.

  1. Roll up a towel and place it on the edge of a step. Stand with the ball of your foot on the towel, so that your toes are bent back, and the heel hanging over the edge. Hold a wall or rail for balance.
  2. Rise slowly onto the ball of the foot, taking about 3 seconds. Pause for 2 seconds at the top.
  3. Lower the heel slowly, taking about 3 seconds.
  • How often: 3 sets of 8 to 12 repetitions, every other day, not daily. The tissue needs a rest day to adapt.
  • Progression: start with both feet if one foot is too hard. When 12 repetitions feel easy, add weight gradually, for example a backpack with a few books, and reduce to fewer repetitions per set.
  • Take extra care, and ask your doctor or physiotherapist first, if you have poor balance, numb feet, poor circulation or diabetes with nerve damage.

5. Short Foot and Towel Scrunches

These two exercises train the small muscles inside the foot that support the arch. The evidence for them is limited, but they are gentle and many people find them useful as a complement to the exercises above.

Short foot

Sitting or standing, keep your toes flat and draw the ball of the foot toward the heel so that the arch lifts, without curling your toes. Hold for 5 seconds, then relax. Repeat 10 times.

Towel scrunch

Sit with your bare foot on a small towel laid flat on a smooth floor. Use your toes to scrunch the towel toward you, then smooth it out again. Repeat 10 to 15 times.

  • How often: once a day.

6. Rolling the Sole of the Foot

Roll the sole of your foot slowly over a massage roller, a ball or a frozen water bottle for 2 to 3 minutes. A cold bottle also gives a pleasant soothing effect. It is not a cure, but many people find it eases soreness, especially after a long day on their feet.

Putting It Together: A Simple Daily Plan

  • On waking, before standing: plantar fascia stretch (1) and towel or band stretch (3).
  • During the day: calf stretches (2), and repeat the plantar fascia stretch after long periods of sitting.
  • Every other day: slow heel raises (4).
  • Once a day: short foot and towel scrunches (5).
  • In the evening: rolling the sole (6).

Start with the lower numbers and build up over a couple of weeks. The whole routine takes about 15 minutes spread over the day.

How Much Pain Is Okay? The “Traffic Light” Rule

  • 🟢 Green light: mild discomfort, up to about 3 out of 10, that settles by the next morning. Safe to continue!
  • 🟡 Yellow light: pain of 4 to 5 out of 10, or a heel that is clearly more painful the next morning. Do fewer repetitions, reduce the weight or skip a day.
  • 🔴 Red light: sharp, stabbing pain, swelling, bruising, or pain that keeps getting worse. Stop, and see a doctor.

Beyond the Exercises

  • Footwear: choose supportive shoes with a cushioned heel, and avoid walking barefoot on hard floors while the pain is active. Keep supportive slippers or sandals next to your bed.
  • Load management: cut back, rather than stop, the activities that aggravate the pain, and build up again gradually. Swimming and cycling are good alternatives.
  • Weight: where relevant, even modest weight loss reduces the load on the fascia.
  • Insoles and night splints: these can help some people, particularly with severe morning pain. Details are in our article on plantar fasciitis and heel spurs.

When to See a Doctor

Do not keep treating yourself at home if you have any of the following:

  • severe pain or swelling, especially after an injury or a fall
  • numbness, tingling or burning in the foot
  • pain at night or at rest that does not ease
  • a red, hot or swollen heel, or fever
  • heel pain in both feet together with morning stiffness in other joints
  • no improvement after 6 to 8 weeks of consistent effort

Other conditions can look like plantar fasciitis, including a stress fracture, a trapped nerve (see nerve pain and neuropathy) and inflammatory arthritis (see rheumatoid arthritis). A proper assessment makes sure you are treating the right problem. For stubborn cases, physiotherapy, shockwave therapy or an injection can be discussed.

The Bottom Line

For plantar fasciitis, steady and regular beats forceful. A few minutes of stretching each day, slow heel raises every other day, good shoes and patience will help most people, even though it takes weeks to months. See the exercises as one part of the treatment, and do not hesitate to ask for help if your heel is not improving.

Always consult your doctor or a physiotherapist before starting a new exercise routine, especially if you have numb feet, balance problems or any worsening of your symptoms.
