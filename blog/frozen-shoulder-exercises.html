Home Exercises for Frozen Shoulder: A Safe, Stage-by-Stage Guide

Frozen shoulder, or adhesive capsulitis, causes stiffness, pain, and reduced range of motion in the shoulder, usually developing gradually over months. Exercise is the cornerstone of recovery — but doing too much, too soon, in an already inflamed shoulder can set you back rather than move you forward. Here are four exercises that help, and how to pace them safely.

Before you start

  • Make sure the diagnosis is confirmed. Shoulder stiffness has several causes — a torn rotator cuff and osteoarthritis can look similar at first — and the right exercise plan depends on knowing which one you're dealing with.
  • Match the intensity to your stage. In the early, painful stage, keep stretches gentle and brief. As the pain eases, you can safely hold stretches longer and go further.
  • Warm up first. A warm shower or a heat pack wrapped in a towel before you start loosens the joint and makes stretching more comfortable.
  • Time your pain relief. If you use painkillers, doing your exercises about an hour after taking them lets you stretch further with less discomfort.

The four exercises

1. Pendulum stretch

Lean forward and support your good arm on a table. Let your frozen arm hang down freely, then gently swing it forward and back, side to side, or in small circles for about a minute. Do this 3 times a day. Keep the circles small while the shoulder is still painful, and widen them gradually as it improves.

2. Wall walk (finger crawl)

Stand facing a wall, about arm's length away. Slowly "walk" your fingers up the wall as high as you comfortably can, then lower your arm back down slowly. Repeat a few times. The fingers should be doing the climbing — if you find yourself hitching your shoulder or leaning your body to gain height, you've gone higher than the joint is ready for.

3. Towel stretch

Hold a towel with both hands behind your back, with your good arm on top, and gently pull the affected arm upward until you feel a light stretch. Hold for 15 seconds and repeat 3 to 5 times. If your shoulder is still in the painful, inflamed stage, shorten this to just a few seconds per hold rather than the full 15 — length of hold matters less than staying pain-free early on.

4. Table slide

Sit at a table with a towel under your hands. Lean forward and slide your hands along the table away from your body, holding the stretch for 15 seconds. As with the towel stretch, keep this brief and gentle if you're still in the painful stage.

How much is too much?

A gentle ache during or after these stretches, which fades within 30 minutes of stopping, is normal and expected — it's a sign the tightened capsule is responding to the stretch. Sharp pain, pain that worsens as you move, or pain that's still there more than an hour later is a different signal: stop, rest, and go gentler next time.

What these four don't cover

Frozen shoulder typically restricts outward rotation more than any other movement, and none of the four stretches above train it directly. Once your pain allows, ask your doctor or physiotherapist about adding an external rotation stretch (pushing a stick or towel across the body with the elbow tucked in) and a cross-body stretch — these fill a real gap in the basic routine.
