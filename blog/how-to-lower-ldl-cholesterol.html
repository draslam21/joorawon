How to Lower Your LDL Cholesterol

Most people can lower their LDL cholesterol meaningfully with changes to what they eat and how they live. LDL is often called “bad” cholesterol because, when too much circulates in your blood, it builds up in the walls of your arteries. Over years, this narrows them and raises the risk of heart attack and stroke.

The difficult part is that high LDL causes no symptoms. You can feel perfectly well while it quietly does damage, which is why knowing your number and acting on it matters.

Know your numbers

A simple blood test called a lipid profile measures your LDL along with your other blood fats. You do not need to be fasting for most people, but follow your doctor's instructions.

There is no single target for everyone. Your ideal LDL depends on your overall risk: age, blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, family history, and whether you have already had a heart attack, stroke or stent. The higher your risk, the lower your LDL should be. As a rough guide, many people without other risk factors aim for below 3.0 mmol/L (about 115 mg/dL), while people with heart disease or diabetes are often advised to aim well below 1.8 mmol/L (about 70 mg/dL), and sometimes lower. Ask your doctor what your own target is.

Heart disease and diabetes are common in Mauritius, so adults should have their cholesterol checked at least once, and more often if they have risk factors or a family history of early heart disease. Very high levels, especially in a young person, can be inherited (familial hypercholesterolaemia) and need specialist attention.

Eat to lower LDL

The biggest dietary lever is the type of fat you eat. Swapping saturated fat for unsaturated fat lowers LDL more reliably than cutting out cholesterol-containing foods like eggs. In Mauritius, much of the saturated fat comes from how food is cooked and what it is served with, rather than from any one dish.

Eat less of:

  • Deep-fried snacks such as gato piment, bajia, samoussa, boulette and fried chicken, especially when the oil has been reused many times
  • Farata, and roti or dholl puri cooked or brushed with a lot of ghee or oil
  • Ghee, butter, margarine, cream, palm oil and coconut oil, and curries made rich with cream or a lot of coconut milk
  • Sausages, salami, chorizo, corned beef, fatty cuts of mutton and beef, and chicken skin
  • Cakes, pastries, croissants and packaged biscuits, which often contain palm oil, margarine or trans fats

Eat more of:

  • Dholl, gram (pois chiche), haricot rouge, pois du cap and other pulses. Their soluble fibre helps your body remove cholesterol. Eat them most days.
  • Brèdes, chouchou, bringelle, lalo (okra), tomatoes and other vegetables, cooked with little oil. Bringelle and lalo are also rich in soluble fibre.
  • Local fruit such as guava, papaya, orange and apple
  • Oats, brown rice, barley and whole-wheat bread or roti in place of white bread and refined flour
  • Fish such as sardines, maquereau (mackerel) and tuna, grilled, steamed or cooked in a light rougaille, about twice a week
  • A small handful of unsalted peanuts, almonds or walnuts a day
  • Soya, sunflower, canola or olive oil, measured with a spoon rather than poured, in place of ghee, butter and solid fats

Simple rules for the kitchen: grill, steam, bake or simmer instead of deep-frying, trim visible fat and remove chicken skin, and fill half your plate with vegetables. Dietary changes alone can often lower LDL by roughly 10 to 15%, and more for people who change a lot (approximate).

Lifestyle habits that help

Diet does most of the work for LDL itself, but other habits protect your heart and support your results.

  • Move more. Aim for at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, such as a brisk walk in the cooler hours of the morning or evening, cycling, swimming or dancing. Exercise has a modest direct effect on LDL, but it supports weight control and overall heart health.
  • Lose excess weight. Even a 5 to 10% loss can improve your cholesterol profile and blood pressure.
  • Stop smoking. Smoking does not raise LDL much, but it damages artery walls and makes LDL more harmful. Quitting is one of the best things you can do for your heart.
  • Limit alcohol. Heavy drinking raises blood pressure and adds empty calories. If you drink, keep it modest.
  • Manage other conditions. Controlling blood pressure and blood sugar lowers your overall risk, which is what ultimately matters.

When to see a doctor

Book a check-up and a lipid test if you have not had one, or if any of these apply:

  • You have diabetes, high blood pressure, kidney disease or you smoke
  • A close relative had a heart attack or stroke at a young age (before about 55 in men, 65 in women)
  • Your LDL is very high, or you were told you have high cholesterol in the past
  • You have had a heart attack, stroke, stent or bypass

Seek emergency care immediately for chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or sudden weakness, facial droop or difficulty speaking. These are not cholesterol symptoms to wait on.

In short

  1. Get your LDL measured and ask what your personal target is.
  2. Swap saturated and fried foods for fibre, fish, nuts and unsaturated oils.
  3. Stay active, reach a healthy weight and do not smoke.
  4. Have your LDL rechecked regularly and review your target with your doctor.
