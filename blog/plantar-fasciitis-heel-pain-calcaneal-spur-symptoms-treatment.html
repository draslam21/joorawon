Heel Pain With the First Steps of the Morning? Plantar Fasciitis & Heel Spurs Explained

Do you dread putting your foot on the floor when you get out of bed? Does your heel feel like it has a stone or a nail in it for the first few steps, then ease off, only to hurt again after a long day on your feet? Were you told you have “a heel spur” and that you will need surgery? This is very likely plantar fasciitis, the most common cause of heel pain. The good news is that it is rarely serious, and most people recover without an operation, although it needs patience.

What Are Plantar Fasciitis and a Heel Spur?

The plantar fascia is a thick, strong band of tissue that runs under your foot, from the heel bone to the base of the toes. It supports the arch and acts like a spring each time you walk or run. When it is overloaded again and again, the area where it attaches to the heel bone becomes irritated and painful. Doctors now think the problem is more a wear-and-overload of the tissue than a simple “inflammation”, which is why you will sometimes hear the term plantar fasciosis or simply plantar heel pain.

A heel spur (calcaneal spur) is a small, bony growth that can form where the fascia attaches to the heel bone. It shows up on an X-ray, and that is where the confusion starts. The spur is usually not the cause of the pain. Many people with a spur have no pain at all, and many people with typical plantar fasciitis have no spur. The spur is a sign that the area has been under strain for a long time, not a thorn pressing into your heel.

The Warning Signs

  • Sharp or stabbing pain under the heel with the first steps in the morning, or after sitting for a while. This is the classic clue.
  • Pain that eases after a few minutes of walking, then returns after long periods standing or walking.
  • Tenderness on the inner, front part of the heel, where the fascia attaches. Pressing there reproduces the pain.
  • Pain that is worse barefoot, on hard floors or tiles, or in flat, thin-soled shoes and flip-flops.
  • Pain that builds up gradually, often after a recent change: more walking, a new job on your feet, a new sport, or weight gain.
  • Usually one heel, sometimes both.

Who Gets It, and Why?

There is rarely a single cause. Several things make it more likely:

  • Age 40 to 60. The fat pad under the heel thins and the tissue becomes less resilient.
  • Excess weight. More load goes through the fascia with every step.
  • Standing or walking for long hours, for example nurses, teachers, factory and shop workers, and drivers.
  • A sudden increase in activity, such as starting running, a new exercise class or a long pilgrimage walk.
  • Tight calf muscles and Achilles tendon, which pull on the heel and limit ankle movement.
  • Flat feet or very high arches, and unsupportive footwear: flip-flops (tongs), worn-out trainers, hard-soled shoes, or walking barefoot on tiled floors.
  • Pregnancy, because of weight gain and loosening of ligaments.
  • Certain medical conditions, such as inflammatory arthritis (see rheumatoid arthritis) and, in some people, diabetes.

Is It Really Plantar Fasciitis? How It Is Diagnosed

Plantar fasciitis is diagnosed mainly from your story and an examination: where the pain is, what it feels like first thing in the morning, and what the doctor finds when pressing on the heel and moving the ankle and toes.

Scans are not usually needed to make the diagnosis. They are used when the picture is unclear, when pain is severe, or when it has not improved after several months:

  • X-ray. Useful to rule out other causes, and it may show a heel spur. Remember that the spur does not confirm the diagnosis and does not predict how bad the pain is.
  • Ultrasound. Can show a thickened plantar fascia, and is a simple way to support the diagnosis.
  • MRI. Reserved for doubtful cases, for example to look for a stress fracture.
  • Blood tests, when a rheumatological cause is suspected, such as pain in both heels, morning stiffness, or other swollen joints.

Not every heel pain is plantar fasciitis. Other causes include:

  • A stress fracture of the heel bone, especially after a sudden increase in activity.
  • Tarsal tunnel syndrome, where a nerve is squeezed behind the ankle, causing burning, tingling or numbness (see nerve pain explained).
  • Achilles tendinopathy, with pain at the back of the heel rather than underneath.
  • Heel fat pad syndrome, with a bruised feeling in the middle of the heel.
  • Inflammatory arthritis such as spondyloarthritis, which can inflame the attachment points at the heel.
  • Gout and, rarely, infection or a bone tumour.

How Long Does It Last?

Plantar fasciitis usually improves over months, not days. With proper treatment, most people get better within 6 to 12 months, and the great majority recover without surgery. The most common reason for it dragging on is returning to the same overload too soon, or giving up the exercises after a couple of weeks when the pain starts to ease.

How Is It Treated?

Treatment is step by step, and starts with simple measures. Complete rest is not the answer: the aim is to reduce the load, then rebuild the strength and flexibility of the foot.

  • Stretching and exercises. This is the foundation of treatment. Regular calf stretches and stretches that target the plantar fascia itself (pulling the toes back toward the shin) are the best-supported measures. Strengthening exercises for the foot and calf are often added. A physiotherapist can guide you.
  • Reducing the load for a while. Cut back on long standing, running and jumping, then return gradually. Swimming and cycling are good alternatives.
  • Supportive footwear. Shoes with a cushioned sole, arch support and a slightly raised heel. Avoid flip-flops, flat thin-soled shoes and walking barefoot on hard floors, even at home.
  • Heel cups, cushioned insoles or orthotics. They can reduce pain and work best alongside stretching, rather than on their own.
  • A night splint. Keeps the foot gently stretched during sleep and can help stubborn, long-standing pain, though some people find it uncomfortable.
  • Weight loss, when needed. Less weight means less load on the fascia with every step.
  • Ice and simple pain relief. Rolling the sole over a cold bottle for 10 minutes can ease the pain. Anti-inflammatory tablets (NSAIDs) may give short-term relief for some, but the evidence for lasting benefit is limited, and they are not suitable for everyone (stomach, kidney, heart and blood pressure problems). Ask your doctor first.
  • Physiotherapy. Manual therapy, taping and guided exercise programmes.
  • Shockwave therapy (ESWT). A reasonable option for pain that has not improved after several months of proper conservative treatment.
  • Corticosteroid injection. Can give relief for several weeks, but it is painful, the benefit is often short-lived, and repeated injections carry risks, including thinning of the heel fat pad and, rarely, rupture of the fascia. It is used selectively, rather than repeated.
  • Surgery. Only rarely needed, for severe pain that has failed 6 to 12 months of well-followed treatment. Removing a heel spur on its own is generally not what fixes the pain.

Some people try magnets, supplements, creams or “miracle” insoles. Evidence for these is limited, so discuss them with your doctor before spending money on them.

Habits That Help

  • Stretch your calves and the soles of your feet before getting out of bed and before long walks.
  • Never take your first steps barefoot on a hard floor: keep supportive slippers or sandals beside your bed.
  • Wear cushioned, supportive shoes for work and exercise, and replace worn-out trainers.
  • Build up activity gradually rather than suddenly doing much more than usual.
  • Keep a healthy weight.
  • If you stand all day, take short sitting breaks and vary your standing surface when possible.
  • Be patient and keep up your exercises even when the pain improves.

When to Seek Medical Help Quickly

  • 🔴 A hot, red, swollen heel with fever, or you cannot put any weight on the foot: infection or a fracture must be ruled out urgently.
  • 🔴 Heel pain after a fall or injury, with swelling, bruising or inability to walk.
  • 🔴 A red, hot, very painful foot or toe that came on suddenly: this could be gout or infection.
  • 🟡 Numbness, tingling or burning in the foot, or pain that wakes you at night.
  • 🟡 Pain in both heels with morning stiffness, or other swollen or painful joints, back pain or a skin or eye problem.
  • 🟡 Pain that has not improved after 6 to 8 weeks of proper treatment, or that keeps getting worse.
  • 🟡 You have diabetes, poor circulation or numb feet, because foot problems need earlier and closer attention. See also the warning signs of diabetes.

The Bottom Line

Plantar fasciitis is common, painful and usually very treatable. Heel spurs are a frequent X-ray finding, but they are rarely the cause of the pain, and they do not mean you need surgery. Stretching, the right footwear, sensible activity and patience bring most people back to walking comfortably without needing an operation.

If your heel pain is limiting your walking or work, or has lasted more than a few weeks, do not wait for it to go away on its own. Always consult your doctor before starting or stopping any medicine.
